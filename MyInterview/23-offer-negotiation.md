# 23 · Offer & Salary Negotiation (8 questions)

[← Panic Sheet](22-panic-sheet.md) · [Home](README.md) · [Next → Role-Tailoring Guide](24-role-tailoring-guide.md)

The compensation conversation is the part most senior people prepare least — and it is the highest-leverage ten minutes of the whole process. Everything here is in my own voice: calm, evidence-based, never adversarial. I negotiate the way I run a project — clear on what matters, honest about trade-offs, and anchored in value delivered.

> **My rule for the money talk:** *be warm, be specific, and never be the first to name a number if I can help it. Anchor on value, not on my current salary.*

## N1 · "What's your current salary?"

**Answer.** I keep this light and redirect to the future, not the past. *"I'd rather focus on the value of this role and what the market rate is for it than anchor on my current package — my current number reflects a different scope. What range have you budgeted for this position?"*

In many places asking is discouraged or not allowed, so a polite deflection is normal and expected. The goal is to move the anchor from *my history* to *this role's worth*.

**Follow-ups**
- *"They insist on a number."* — I give total compensation (base + bonus + benefits), not just base, so the figure reflects real value — then immediately pivot to the range for this role.
- *"Won't deflecting annoy them?"* — Not if I'm warm and give them something in return — my expectation range. It reads as professional, not evasive.

---

## N2 · "What's your salary expectation?"

**Answer.** I answer with a researched range, not a single number, and I put my target near the *bottom* of the range so there is room to settle up. *"Based on the scope — a hands-on solution architect owning design and delivery — and the market, I'm looking in the range of X to Y, depending on the total package."*

I base the range on real research (market data for the role, level and location) so it is defensible, not a wish. And I always tie it back to scope: I'm pricing the *value* I bring, not a percentage bump on my last job.

**Follow-ups**
- *"How did you get that range?"* — Market data for the role and location, adjusted for the seniority and the hands-on-plus-architecture scope. I can walk them through it.
- *"Your range is above budget."* — Then let's talk about scope and the total package — base, bonus, equity, and where there's flexibility. I'm solving for the whole picture.

---

## N3 · "The offer is below target — how do you counter?"

**Answer.** I stay warm and appreciative first, then counter with evidence, never with emotion. *"Thank you — I'm genuinely excited about this. Based on the scope and my research, I was expecting closer to X. Can we get there?"* Then I stop talking and let the silence do the work.

I counter once, clearly, with a specific number and a reason — not a vague "can you do better". And I make it easy to say yes by showing I'm negotiating in good faith, not haggling for sport.

**Follow-ups**
- *"What if they say no to base?"* — I move to the rest of the package — see [N5](#n5--what-if-they-cant-move-on-base). There is almost always a lever that isn't base.
- *"How hard do you push?"* — One firm, respectful counter. If they genuinely can't move, I decide on the whole offer — I don't burn goodwill over the last 2%.

---

## N4 · "Why do you deserve that much?"

**Answer.** I anchor on impact I've delivered, with numbers — the same evidence discipline as the rest of my interview. *"On my last platform I cut manual effort 60%, halved the release cycle, and shipped the firm's first production RAG app. I don't just design — I build the critical path and own it in production. That combination is what the number reflects."*

I'm not asking for a favour; I'm pricing a proven outcome. A hands-on architect who de-risks the hard decisions early and writes the reusable patterns is a force-multiplier, not a cost line.

**Follow-ups**
- *"Everyone claims impact."* — Mine is measured and traceable to a project — I can name the platform, the metric and how it was measured.
- *"What's your single strongest justification?"* — I own design *and* I ship production code, so you get an architect and a senior builder in one hire.

---

## N5 · "What if they can't move on base?"

**Answer.** I widen the conversation beyond base salary — there is usually flex somewhere even when base is capped. I have a ranked list ready so I trade for what matters most to me.

The levers I reach for, roughly in order: **sign-on bonus** (bridges a base gap in year one), **annual bonus target or equity**, **an early review** (e.g. a 6-month comp review tied to goals), **extra leave**, **remote/flex arrangement**, **learning/certification budget**, and **title/scope** if it affects my next move.

**Follow-ups**
- *"Which lever matters most to you?"* — I decide that *before* the call so I'm not improvising — usually a sign-on plus an early review beats a small base bump.
- *"Isn't a one-off bonus worse than base?"* — Long-term yes, so I pair a sign-on with a documented early comp review, turning a one-off into a path.

---

## N6 · "Do you have other offers?"

**Answer.** I'm honest but strategic. If I have a competing offer, I mention it factually as context, not as a threat. *"I am in a late stage with one other firm, but this role is my strong preference — I'd like to make it work."* If I don't, I don't invent one — a bluff that unravels destroys trust.

The leverage isn't the other offer itself; it's that I'm clearly a wanted, in-demand candidate who still prefers *them*. That's a much stronger position than a bidding war.

**Follow-ups**
- *"No other offer — are you weak then?"* — No. My leverage is the value and evidence I bring; the process should price that regardless of a rival bid.
- *"They ask you to name the other company/number."* — I keep it confidential — *"I'd rather not share specifics, but I'm being transparent that this is my preferred role."*

---

## N7 · "They pressure you to decide today."

**Answer.** I stay calm and ask for reasonable time — a real offer survives a night's sleep. *"I'm very keen and want to say yes properly — could I have until [a specific day] to review the full details?"* A genuine employer will grant a day or two; heavy pressure to sign *now* is itself a small red flag.

I never let urgency push me into a number I'll resent. A rushed yes becomes a quiet no six months later.

**Follow-ups**
- *"What's a reasonable ask?"* — Usually 2–5 business days. I name a specific date so it reads as committed, not stalling.
- *"The offer 'expires' tonight."* — I'd ask why, politely. Artificial deadlines tell me something about how they'll operate later.

---

## N8 · "How do you accept or decline?"

**Answer.** **Accepting:** I get the final offer *in writing* before I resign anything, confirm I've read every term (comp, bonus mechanics, start date, notice), and reply warmly. Same rule as my proposals — *I never let a commitment be signed that I have not read* ([R5](06-rfp-presales.md#r5--how-do-you-work-with-sales-commercial-and-legal)).

**Declining:** I do it graciously and promptly — thank them, keep the door open, never burn the bridge. The person I decline today may be the client or referrer I need in two years. A short, kind note is remembered.

**Follow-ups**
- *"Verbal offer — good enough?"* — No. I'm warm and grateful, but I wait for it in writing before I act on it.
- *"Counter-offer from your current employer?"* — I weigh it honestly, but remember *why* I was leaving — money rarely fixes the real reason.
